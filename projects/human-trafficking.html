Human Trafficking

Human trafficking has been a complicated global crime because of different social, demographic, and economic factors. It is a sensitive and often under-reported topic. This project will analyze the patterns in human trafficking cases by looking at exploitation types, victim age distribution over time, and relationships between victims and exploiters across age groups.

Methodology and Data Sources

Data are drawn from The Counter Trafficking Data Collaborative (CTDC) global dataset, which comprises identified or self-reported cases of victims of trafficking globally from 2005 to 2018. All observations with missing values for age, exploitation type, or exploiter relationship were excluded for better consistency, but this process may also introduce bias by under-representing cases where data collection is incomplete.

Measure Descriptions

The analysis focuses on three variables related to human trafficking:

  1. Victim's age: Age of reporting individuals when they reported their trafficking case.
  2. Exploitation type: The kind of exploitation victims experienced, including sexual exploitation, forced labor, forced marriage, slavery, and other kinds of exploitation.
  3. Exploiter relationship: The relationship between the exploiter and the victim, including family/relative, friend/acquaintance, intimate partner, and other kinds of relationship.

Note that the year described in the dataset is the year reported, not necessarily the year the exploitation took place.

Comparative Trends and Analysis

The distribution of exploitation types shows that sexual exploitation and forced labor are the most commonly reported cases, accounting for the majority of trafficking incidents, and sexual exploitation is the most prevalent. Other categories such as forced marriage and slavery make up a smaller proportion of cases. The dominance of sexual exploitation and forced labor suggest that human trafficking is much driven by demand in labor and commercial sex markets.

Examining the age distribution of victims, there is significant variation across age groups over time. Adolescents aged 9–17 consistently represent one of the largest shares of victims, indicating vulnerability among minors. Young children aged 0–8 appear less frequently in reported cases, though this may reflect underreporting or one's inability to report at such a young age rather than true incidence. Adult age groups, especially those between 21 and 38, show changing but strong representation. Thus, trafficking affects both minors and working-age adults. Over time, there are moderate shifts rather than dramatic structural changes in age distribution.

The analysis of exploiter relationships reflects social dynamics. For younger victims, particularly those aged 0–17, exploiters are more likely to be family members or relatives, possibly due to the role of trust and proximity in achieving exploitation. As age increases, the proportion of non-familial exploiters such as acquaintances becomes more prominent, possibly due to more impersonal or organized trafficking networks. Notably, intimate partner exploitation appears across many age groups, though it is rarely the most dominant one. This shows that trafficking happens within romantic relationships, which complicates institutional prevention efforts or policy.

Literature Review and National Context

Different from other crimes, human trafficking requires global efforts in prevention because of cross-border trafficking networks. International organizations aim to document broader global trends. Based on data from 155 countries, women and girls are the majority of detected trafficking victims, particularly in cases of sexual exploitation (United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2019). Forced labor has been increasing rapidly, especially among adult victims, which reflects global labor market inequalities and systemic migration issues. Children are also disproportionately affected in certain regions, such as parts of Africa and Asia, validating our findings that young people are vulnerable to risks of trafficking.

The countries that implemented the United Nations Protocol against Trafficking in Persons doubled in the past few years, but there are still many countries that lack legal instruments or political will against trafficking.

Since the 21st century, there is more international awareness in preventing human trafficking. A 2025 global operation identified over 1,100 potential victims and led to more than 150 arrests; while there is strong persistence of trafficking networks, international cooperation in disrupting them is important. These operations also reveal that trafficking increasingly involves organized criminal networks, particularly in labor exploitation cases (INTERPOL, 2025).

One's social and economic vulnerability are also key drivers of trafficking. Traffickers often use existing trusting relationships to control and exploit victims, as shown in our findings, and areas where people experience poverty, lack of education, and gender inequality are susceptible to trafficking because of that.

In conclusion, human trafficking cannot be explained by a single factor, but it is rather a complex crime shaped by demographic vulnerabilities, social relationships, and global economic factors. Preventive policies must combine targeted protection for high-risk groups, stronger labor protections, and international collaboration to disrupt trafficking networks.
