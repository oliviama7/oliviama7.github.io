Gun Violence in the U.S.

Gun violence is an important metric for evaluating both the effectiveness of gun control laws and the socio-economic conditions of a region. This project compares state-level gun violence incidence rates, examines patterns within Illinois from 2014 to 2017, and considers disparities in gun regulation across states and between countries.

Methodology and Data Sources

Data are drawn from the Gun Violence Archive, which provides comprehensive statistics on gun-related incidents in the United States from 2014 to 2017. To ensure comparability across states, incidence rates are standardized as per 100,000 residents. Population data are sourced from the U.S. Census Bureau's state population totals.

Measure Descriptions

The analysis focuses on two measures related to gun violence:

  1. Gun violence incidents: Events resulting in injury or fatality caused by a firearm. Incidents without injury or fatality are excluded.
  2. Gun violence incidence rate: The number of gun violence incidents per 100,000 residents.

Comparative Trends and Analysis

From 2014 to 2017, gun violence incidence rates show clear regional differences across the nation. Southern and Midwestern states consistently report higher rates, with Louisiana and Illinois reaching 37.1 and 35.9 incidents per 100,000 residents, respectively, in 2017. In contrast, Western and Northeastern states maintain comparatively lower rates. While these regional patterns remain stable over time, most states experienced an overall increase in incidence rates during this period.

Within Illinois, gun-related incidents show distinct temporal patterns. Incidents occur more frequently on weekends and between April and October, with Sundays in August recording the highest number of incidents (378 cases across the observed years). These patterns may reflect increased social activity and mobility during weekends and warmer months, which can increase the likelihood of interpersonal conflict and criminal activity.

Across all states, the top ten highest incidence rates from 2014 to 2017 consistently include Louisiana, Illinois, Delaware, Mississippi, Alabama, Tennessee, Missouri, South Carolina, Maryland, and Alaska. Illinois remains among the top three throughout this period. Notably, Illinois enforces relatively strict gun control laws, including the Firearm Owner's Identification (FOID) card, Concealed Carry Licenses (CCL), and regulations on firearm transport (Koester, 2025). However, these restrictions may contribute to unintended consequences, such as the illegal trafficking of firearms from neighboring states with more lenient regulations (Bowman, 2018). This complicates gun-related law enforcement, as illegal firearms are more difficult to track and regulate. Additionally, high-density urban areas such as the Chicago metropolitan region likely contribute to increased incidence rates because of population concentration and increased social interaction.

Literature Review and National Context

A key disparity between the United States and Canada lies in how gun ownership is conceptualized and regulated (Lukiv, 2026). In the United States, gun ownership is constitutionally protected under the Second Amendment, limiting the extent to which federal and state governments can impose gun control laws. In contrast, Canada treats gun ownership as a regulated privilege, requiring licensing, safety training, and comprehensive background checks. Certain firearms, including assault-style weapons, are banned, and there is even a national freeze on handgun sales.

This fundamental difference shapes policy flexibility and gun violence outcomes. In Canada, policy responses could be more centralized and aggressive in gun control, whereas U.S. policymakers face legal and political constraints. These differences are reflected in outcomes: mass shootings are significantly less frequent in Canada, and about 38% of homicides involve firearms, compared to 78.5% in the United States.

Both countries have implemented gun buyback programs aimed at reducing the number of firearms in circulation; however, their effectiveness remains limited. In the United States, such programs are often ineffective due to the highly elastic supply of guns, meaning that reductions in supply through buybacks do not actually change market incentives. Similarly, a Canadian pilot buyback program that aimed to collect 200 firearms ultimately collected only 26.

Overall, regional disparities, structural enforcement challenges, and underlying regulatory frameworks highlight the complexity of addressing gun violence in the United States. Policy effectiveness depends not only on regulation but also on social, economic, and political conditions.
